📋 HTML report anatomy

From the 60-second summary to verified references — the briefing sections and what each one delivers.

0 of 6
What it is:

The first section of the briefing. It starts with the established fact and only then presents the contested interpretation—with nuance, not a headline.

Why learn:

This is what the reader sees first; the decision needs to fit in 60 seconds.

Key concepts:

Established fact · contested interpretation · decision level.

What it is:

The most important findings, ranked by reliability; each with a score from 1 to 10 and “Supported by” / “Challenged by” chips from the lenses.

Why learn:

The order and the note tell you what to trust first.

Key concepts:

Ranking · score 1–10 · supports/challenges.

What it is:

The non-obvious link that appears only when you cross-check all five lenses—it comes directly from the contradiction map.

Why learn:

This is the insight that no single lens can see.

Key concepts:

Cross-check · non-obvious link · synthesis.

What it is:

What no lens addressed, framed as the lens that could change the conclusions.

Why learn:

Shows the panel’s limits and informs the boundary question.

Key concepts:

Blind spot · sixth lens · limit.

What it is:

From 3 to 6 specific actions for the reader’s role defined in Phase 0—concrete, not abstract.

Why learn:

This is where the research turns into action for you.

Key concepts:

Actionable · target reader · specific.

What it is:

The guide to what to claim, qualify, and avoid; the boundary question that would change everything; and the references, each with a status tag.

Why learn:

Wraps up the report by saying what it’s safe to claim and where the limit is.

Key concepts:

Claim safety · boundary question · reference with status.

🔎 The verification system

How STORM proves the numbers are real — banner, status tags for each citation, and the reliability hierarchy.

0 of 6
What it is:

The badge at the top of the report: “X fabricated, Y corrected, Z downgraded” and the score “N/N checked.” It has to be true.

Why learn:

This is the one-line proof that Phase 4 actually ran.

Key concepts:

Banner · N/N score · honesty.

What it is:

Each citation gets a tag: CONFIRMED, PARTIALLY CONFIRMED, CORRECTED, DOWNGRADED, UNVERIFIED, or FALSE.

Why learn:

You see each source’s verdict without redoing the check.

Key concepts:

Verdict · status · traceability.

What it is:

How the 1-to-10 score comes from source quality: peer-reviewed causal > official data > single study > analogy > preprint.

Why learn:

Recaps 1.3 with examples and explains why one finding gets a 9 and another gets a 4.

Key concepts:

Hierarchy · note · source quality.

What it is:

Preprints and commissioned studies are downgraded; the statistic from a single study is honestly attributed to its source.

Why learn:

Avoids treating weak evidence as if it were strong.

Key concepts:

Preprint · commissioned study · attribution.

What it is:

The sidebar where disputed claims and preprints go, always with the strongest opposing source alongside.

Why learn:

Separate what’s solid from what’s still disputed.

Key concepts:

Contested signal · opposing source · transparency.

What it is:

Even when verified, the panel was written by the same author; check the numbers that actually matter to your decision.

Why learn:

Verification reduces error, not your responsibility.

Key concepts:

Authorial convergence · critical reading · responsibility.

🧩 Customize and extend

The skill is yours: add lenses, tailor it to the reader, switch the model for a subagent, and carry the STORM principles forward.

0 of 6
What it is:

You add a new lens (e.g., "THE AI BEGINNER" or "THE CONTENT CREATOR") following the template of the five—with the 3 required outputs.

Why learn:

Adapts the panel to your domain without breaking the method.

Key concepts:

Lens patch · 3 required outputs · template.

What it is:

Preload context about your business and your role into the skill so every report is tailored to you.

Why learn:

The actionable insight is much more useful when it’s aimed at the reader.

Key concepts:

Tailored · reader’s role · context.

What it is:

Where you can safely customize the HTML template while keeping the CSS and identity—without changing the entire visual style.

Why learn:

Customize the output without rewriting the template.

Key concepts:

Template · CSS preserved · identity.

What it is:

Run each lens on a different model based on cost and quality—the expensive one where it matters, the cheap one where it’s enough.

Why learn:

Balances research cost and depth.

Key concepts:

Model by agent · cost/quality · trade-off.

What it is:

The same skill fits in .codex/ or .agents/; only the folder changes; the method stays the same.

Why learn:

You’re not limited to a single agent.

Key concepts:

Portable · .codex · .agents.

What it is:

Apply the idea — multiple perspectives + verification — in other workflows, such as a council or an idea roast.

Why learn:

The value is in the method, not just this skill.

Key concepts:

Multiple perspectives · verification · council/roast.

🧭 Best practices and pitfalls

The method’s guardrails—what never to skip, what never to inflate, and the checklist to use before trusting and sharing.

0 of 6
What it is:

A report delivered without adversarial review and citation checks simply isn’t a STORM report.

Why learn:

This is the rule that protects confidence in the result.

Key concepts:

Phase 4 · mandatory verification · accurate banner.

What it is:

The limit is five lenses and one verifier per citation cluster; more agents don’t mean more truth.

Why learn:

Avoids cost and noise without improving quality.

Key concepts:

Agent cap · cluster · no bloat.

What it is:

Every lens and citation points to a real source that was consulted; anything that can’t be verified is downgraded or cut—never disguised.

Why learn:

This is what separates research from plausible invention.

Key concepts:

Real source · downgrade/cut · no disguising it.

What it is:

Always state in the report that the five lenses have the same author; agreement is a strong hypothesis, not a consensus in the field.

Why learn:

Intellectual honesty is what sustains the method.

Key concepts:

Original panel · convergence · consensus.

What it is:

A run creates about 9 to 11 agents (5 perspectives + 4 to 6 verifiers); predictable, but it needs to be managed.

Why learn:

You decide when and how many times to run it.

Key concepts:

~9–11 agents · predictable · rate-limited.

What it is:

The final check before using the briefing: Phase 4 is complete, the banner is accurate, the panel is disclosed as original work, and you've checked the numbers that matter.

Why learn:

Wraps up the course by turning the method into a habit.

Key concepts:

Checklist · trust · share.
